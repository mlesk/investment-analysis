**Primary framework:** FCFF DCF, supplemented by reverse DCF, five-scenario valuation, sensitivity analysis, and relative valuation.

> **Bottom line:** Netflix is no longer the hyper-growth subscription story of the 2010s — it is a mature, extraordinarily profitable global entertainment franchise whose economics now drive the valuation: ~$48.4B TTM revenue, ~30% operating margins, ~$9–10B normalized free cash flow, and a dominant position in a structurally growing global streaming market. But the market has not yet fully accepted what "mature" means for the discount rate. My base DCF produces approximately **$63/share** at a 9.0% WACC, and probability-weighted value is approximately **$70/share**. At ~$78, the stock trades **above my base case and modestly above my probability-weighted value** — the price still embeds roughly an 8% discount rate, ~13–14% near-term growth, or ~40% terminal margins, which is a plausible but optimistic combination. This is a wonderful business, but at this price the margin of safety is negative. My preferred entry range is **$50–60**. **Rating: C — Hold / Watch.**

The defining feature of Netflix today is the **tension between a superb operating reality and a price that still carries part of the old growth-stock expectations**. Revenue grew 16% last year, operating margin reached 29.5% (vs. 17.8% in 2022), free cash flow is a genuine ~$10B annually, and management has guided to 31.5% margins and ~$12.5B FCF for 2026 — yet the stock has de-rated by ~38% because the market has begun asking a different question: _how much growth is realistically left?_ That question is exactly the right one to ask, and it is the question this valuation answers.

# 1. What Netflix Actually Is

The simplest description:

> **Netflix sells access to one of the world's largest libraries of entertainment — series, films, games, live events, and now advertising — for a monthly subscription, in ~190 countries, to an audience "approaching 1B people."** ([SEC][3])

Revenues are almost entirely monthly membership fees. Members pay a wide range of prices — from the U.S. dollar equivalent of $1 to $38 per month, plus $2–10 for extra-member sub-accounts — and the company additionally monetizes through advertising, consumer products and live experiences. ([SEC][1])

The economic structure matters enormously for valuation:

- **Revenue drivers:** memberships (~over 300M subscribers) × price × plan mix, plus a fast-growing advertising layer (~$3B guided for 2026, roughly doubling year over year). ([SEC][3])
- **Unit economics:** subscription revenue is recognized ratably; content cost is the dominant expense and is largely fixed in the short run.
- **Capital intensity:** unusually low once content spend stabilizes — content additions run near amortization (~1.1x cash spend to amortization guided for 2026), and property/equipment CapEx is only ~$0.7–0.8B/year, ~1.6% of revenue. ([SEC][3], [SEC][2])
- **Tax economics:** effective rate of 13.7–18%, below the 21% federal statutory rate due to FDII, R&D credits and stock-compensation tax benefits. ([SEC][1], [SEC][2])
- **Financing structure:** modest gross debt ($14.3B notes at 3.6–6.4% fixed coupons, plus $2.3B leases) against $9.1B cash; net debt only ~$5.2B (company definition) to ~$7.5B including leases. ([SEC][1])
- **Competitive dynamics:** oligopoly — Netflix competes with Disney, WBD/Max, Amazon Prime Video, YouTube, Paramount and regional players, but is the only streaming player with both global scale and stand-alone profitability.

# 2. The Fundamental Story

> **Netflix will create value because it has already won the global streaming competition and can now convert an enormous, engaged subscriber base into compounding free cash flow through pricing power, margin expansion, and a new advertising business — with minimal incremental capital.**

The three Damodaran variables:

### Cash flows

Netflix generated $10.1B operating cash flow and ~$9.5B FCF in 2025 (my calc: OCF $10,149M − CapEx $688M). Management guides to ~$12.5B FCF for 2026, though that includes ~$2.8B of WBD termination-fee cash; normalized 2026 FCF is ~$10B and rising. ([SEC][2], [SEC][3])

### Growth

Revenue +16% in 2025 and +13% in Q2 2026 (+12% FX-neutral). 2026 guidance is 13–14% growth ($51.0–51.4B), driven by membership growth, price increases, and a doubling of ads revenue. Q3'26 is guided to +11.7%. Growth is decelerating structurally: UCAN grew only 10% in Q2 (and that included a price increase), while LATAM (+21%) and APAC (+16%) still compound. ([SEC][3])

### Risk

Moderate for a media company but real: content cost inflation, competition for viewing time (YouTube, TikTok, AI-generated content), the consumer-discretionary nature of subscriptions, 57% of revenue in foreign currencies, regulatory levies, and a demonstrated management appetite for mega-M&A (the attempted $72B WBD deal). The 52-week price range of $65–$127 shows the market has not settled on how to price these risks. ([SEC][1], [Nasdaq][9])

Netflix is a **mature** company in lifecycle terms — the model must use a two-stage structure: a decade of decelerating growth into stable state, not perpetual double-digit compounding.

What actually caused revenue to grow: (1) global membership growth — though the company stopped disclosing membership counts in 2025 in favor of revenue and margin; (2) **price increases** — H1 2026 price changes in the US, Mexico and Spain "have gone well," with impact consistent with prior changes; (3) **paid-sharing monetization** of existing households; and (4) the emerging **advertising** layer. UCAN growth has slowed to ~10% (half of which is pricing), while EMEA/LATAM/APAC still grow 14–21%. View hours grew only +2% in H1 2026 — the audience is saturated; **future revenue growth must come from pricing, ads and international ARPU, not from adding hundreds of millions of new members.** ([SEC][3])

### Profitability — the most important trend in the company

| Metric              |  2022 |  2023 |  2024 |  2025 |   H1'26 |
| ------------------- | ----: | ----: | ----: | ----: | ------: |
| Operating margin    | 17.8% | 20.6% | 26.7% | 29.5% |   32.8% |
| Net margin          |  ~14% |  ~16% | 22.3% | 24.3% | 35.0%\* |

Margins have risen ~1,000 basis points in three years on scale, content-discipline and cost leverage. 2026 guidance is 31.5% operating margin (vs. 29.5% in 2025), implying operating income growth of 20%+ on 13–14% revenue growth. Management's internal target process ("grow our business globally within the parameters of our operating margin target") has, so far, been executed almost mechanically. ([SEC][3])

### Capital intensity & reinvestment

Netflix's crucial economic fact is **how little incremental capital its growth now requires**:

- FY2025 additions to content assets: $17.10B; content amortization: $16.42B → **net content reinvestment ≈ $0.7B**.
- FY2025 CapEx: $0.69B (1.5% of revenue); depreciation $0.33B.
- Content assets on the balance sheet: $33.8B (net) against $25.1B of content obligations, of which $19.6B is off-balance-sheet. ([SEC][1], [SEC][2])

Cash content spend runs near amortization (guided ~1.1x for 2026), which means **almost all of operating income converts to free cash flow**. FY25: operating income $13.3B → FCF $9.5B, a ~71% conversion ratio, extraordinary for any media company. ([SEC][2], [SEC][3])

### ROIC vs WACC

- ROIC on conventional invested capital (equity + net debt, excluding content assets): **~32%** (consistent with StockAnalysis's 32.4% TTM figure). ([StockAnalysis][5])
- ROIC on all-in invested capital including net content assets (~$67B): **~17%** (NOPAT ~$11.7B ÷ ~$67B).

Either way, ROIC is **far above** my 9.0% WACC. The spread is not marginal: it is one of the widest, most durable spreads in the S&P 500. **This is a value-creating business by any reasonable test** — the valuation question is never whether Netflix creates value, but at what price that value creation is being purchased.

# 4. Competitive Advantage & Industry Economics

### Industry structure

Global streaming is an oligopoly with rational economics: Netflix, Disney (Disney+/Hulu/ESPN), WBD/Max, Amazon Prime Video, YouTube, and regional players. Netflix has ~300M+ subscribers versus roughly 150–170M for Disney+ (estimate), and crucially is the only major pure-play streamer with **stand-alone profitability and scale**: Disney earns a 15.6% operating margin across a conglomerate that includes declining linear networks (TTM revenue $98.9B, net income $8.6B, ROIC 7.1%), while WBD loses money outright (TTM revenue $36.1B, operating margin 7.5%, net loss −$3.2B, ROIC 4.3%). ([StockAnalysis][6], [StockAnalysis][7])

### The moat — and it is real

1. **Scale economics in content:** Netflix spends ~$17B/year on content, ~2.4x Disney's streaming content budget and ~3x WBD's; the amortization base is spread across a much larger revenue pool, structurally lowering unit costs.
2. **The recommendation engine and distribution** (Open Connect CDN, AWS-based compute, product quality) — a genuine data/technology advantage accumulated over 25 years.
3. **Brand + habitual engagement:** 97B viewing hours in H1 2026; ~2 hours/day per member (estimate); the service is culturally embedded in dozens of countries.
4. **Global distribution reach** no competitor can replicate without a decade of losses.

> Does the advantage translate into numbers? Yes — **~30% operating margins and ~17–32% ROIC vs. breakeven-to-single-digit economics for the entire peer set.** The moat is confirmed by observable economics, not narrative.

### Excess returns

ROIC − WACC ≈ 17% − 9% ≈ **800–2,300 bps**, depending on how content assets are treated. Is it widening? Yes — margins are still expanding. How long can it persist? A long time, because the moat is reinforced by the same scale that makes competitive entry economically irrational. The honest caveats: YouTube is a powerful substitute for viewing time; AI-generated content may compress the cost of competition; and ad-tier competition for ad budgets is intense.

**Competitive Advantage Rating: Strong (not "exceptional" — the industry is still contested and content tastes are fickle).**

# 5. Management & Capital Allocation

### Rating: Good to Excellent, with one meaningful reservation

**What management has done right — the observable economics:**

- **Margin discipline:** +1,200 bps of operating margin in four years, with 2026 guided at 31.5% and consensus 2026 operating income growth of 20%+.
- **Capital allocation:** $9.1B of buybacks in 2025 (86.5M shares), $6.0B in H1 2026 (66.4M shares — including the largest buyback quarter in company history, $4.7B in Q2), a new $25B authorization in April 2026 with $27.1B remaining. Shares outstanding have fallen ~1.5% year over year and the buybacks are concentrated at prices ($79–97) well below the 52-week high of $126.71 — **accretive buying at de-rated prices**. ([SEC][1], [SEC][2], [SEC][3])
- **Cash-return economics:** ~$9–10B normalized FCF against a $325B market cap ≈ 3% yield, plus ~1.5% buyback-driven per-share shrinkage.
- **Rational refusal to chase bad deals:** the $2.8B termination fee received when WBD walked into Paramount Skydance's arms means Netflix got paid to _not_ do a deal it might regret. ([SEC][1])

**The reservation:** the WBD episode itself. Netflix signed an $82.7B enterprise-value agreement in December 2025 to buy WBD's streaming and studios businesses, lined up $42.2B of bridge commitments, and had it amended and restated in January 2026 before WBD terminated on February 27, 2026. That management — with a demonstrated 20-year record of patient organic execution — was willing to lever the company into a massive, integration-heavy legacy-media acquisition is a **meaningful risk factor for future capital allocation**. The market punished the announcement period, and the episode raises the question Damodaran always asks: _does management understand its cost of capital?_ The answer, post-WBD, is "probably, but prove it again." ([SEC][1], [SEC][2])

**Incentives:** SBC is modest (~0.8% of revenue: $368M FY25, $272M H1'26); options are granted to employees monthly with 10-year terms, mostly vested; executives hold equity and sell via 10b5-1 plans (Sarandos up to 643K shares; Peters up to 2.95M). No dividend — buybacks are the return channel, which aligns with intrinsic-value thinking. ([SEC][1], [SEC][2])

**Management Quality Rating: Good to Excellent.**

# 6. Accounting & Earnings Normalization

Netflix's accounts are unusually clean for a media company, but two items need care and one needs normalization:

### 1. Content amortization — the critical accounting judgment

Content is capitalized and amortized on an accelerated basis over the shorter of license window, estimated period of use, or ten years; over 90% of an asset amortizes within four years. The amortization schedule is management-estimated from viewing patterns — it is the company's critical audit matter (EY flagged it) and the single biggest lever on reported margins. Netflix has never had a material content write-down event. ([SEC][2])

For valuation: amortization ≈ cash content spend (guided ~1.1x ratio), so **content accounting does not distort FCFF** — the cash-flow statement is the economic truth.

### 2. Stock-based compensation

SBC of $368M in FY2025 (0.8% of revenue) is economically real but modest. Options outstanding: ~124M shares (all vested/exercisable) at a weighted exercise price of $39.21 — meaning ~$4.8B of aggregate intrinsic value (~1.5% of market cap) that will dilute via exercise. I use 4,164M shares outstanding (vs. 4,261M diluted weighted) and treat the difference as dilution conservatism. ([SEC][1])

### 3. One-time items to normalize out of TTM earnings

- **+$2.8B WBD termination fee** (Q1 2026, pre-tax, in "Interest and other income") — inflates TTM net income by ~$2.3B after tax. ([SEC][1])
- **$619M Brazil non-income tax accrual** (Q3 2025) — a real cost, but lumpy.
- **$85M debt-issuance cost write-off** related to the terminated WBD financing (Q1 2026 interest expense). ([SEC][1])
- FX remeasurement noise on Euro-denominated notes (hedged but imperfectly).

**Normalized TTM figures I use:** revenue $48.4B; operating income ~$14.4B (29.7% margin); normalized net income ≈ $11.3B (ex-fee); normalized FCF ≈ $8.8–9.0B TTM (ex-fee), with management's ~$10B run-rate for 2026 the better forward anchor. Effective tax rate: 16% (Q2'26) to 17.2% (TTM) — I use 16%. ([SEC][1], [StockAnalysis][5])

**Do not use:** TTM EPS of $3.17 or TTM net income of $13.65B — both include the $2.8B fee. ([StockAnalysis][5])

# 7. Growth Analysis

### Decomposition

- **Volume:** memberships still grow (LATAM +21% revenue, APAC +16% in Q2) but UCAN is saturated; view hours +2%.
- **Pricing:** the largest lever left; recent US/Mexico/Spain increases "consistent with prior changes and expectations."
- **New revenue:** advertising — guided to ~$3B in 2026 (roughly doubling), with upfront commitments nearly doubled and AI-powered ad tools scaling; ~5x to 10x growth over the decade is conceivable but unproven.
- **Adjacencies:** live events (NFL, MLB, WWE, Women's World Cup, boxing), cloud games (FIFA, Unhinged), video podcasts, publisher partnerships (TF1), merchandise.

### Fundamental growth

Expected growth = reinvestment rate × ROIC. Netflix's actual reinvestment rate is tiny (~10–15% of NOPAT: ~$1.4–3B of net content + CapEx reinvestment against ~$12B NOPAT) because content spend ≈ amortization. Applied mechanically with a 17–32% ROIC, fundamental growth is **5–15% — comfortably supportive of high-single-digit revenue growth, and this is the mathematical core of why Netflix can compound earnings ~15–20% annually (growth + buybacks + margin) without heroic assumptions.**

### Growth quality

**High quality.** Growth requires almost no incremental capital — it is pricing, ads and margin-mix, not balance-sheet expansion.

### Growth duration

High growth is **over** in the subscriber sense; what remains is a long tail of mid-single-digit revenue growth plus margin expansion plus buybacks. That is still a powerful compounding machine — but it is a different machine from the one that justified 50–90x earnings. My base case assumes revenue growth decays from ~10% next year to ~3.2% by 2036.

**What causes growth to slow:** audience saturation (~1B people reached), competition for viewing time, and the arithmetic of large numbers on pricing.

# 8. Risk Analysis

### Business risk (moderate)

- **Competition:** YouTube, Disney, Amazon, and free/AI-generated content compete for the same hours; content costs could re-inflate (2023 strikes, sports rights).
- **Consumer cyclicality:** subscriptions are discretionary; 2022's subscriber declines showed churn sensitivity to price and macro.
- **Concentration in content taste:** a cold slate means churn (engagement growth only +2%).
- **Regulatory:** EU cultural levies, content quotas, Brazil non-income tax (the $619M accrual), ad/privacy regulation.
- **FX:** 57% of revenue non-USD; hedged but not eliminated. ([SEC][1])

### Financial risk (low)

Debt $14.3B at fixed 3.6–6.4% coupons, maturities 2026–2054, vs. $9.1B cash and ~$10B FCF; interest coverage ~16x; a $3B revolver and $3B CP program undrawn. EBITDA/interest covenant of 3.0x is covered many times over. **Financial distress probability: negligible.**

### Valuation risk (the one that matters)

The market cap is ~$325B and the value is highly sensitive to the discount rate, terminal margin and growth duration (Sections 12–14). The stock's 52-week range ($65–$127) is the market itself telling you the uncertainty around these inputs is large.

### Failure risk

Permanent impairment probability: low. Business-model failure: very low. The realistic failure mode is **opportunity cost** — paying 24x earnings for 10% compounding when you could earn more elsewhere — plus a tail of value-destructive M&A.

# 11. Terminal Value

- **Stable growth: 3.0%** (≤ long-run nominal GDP; below the 4.74% risk-free rate).
- **Stable ROIC: 20%** → stable reinvestment rate = 3%/20% = 15%.
- **Stable operating margin: ~36.5%**, reached at the end of the explicit period.
- **Stable WACC: 9.0%.**

Terminal value is **63% of enterprise value**. That is high — typical for a mature, low-reinvestment compounder — and it means the entire valuation rests on three terminal judgments: (1) Netflix still has a moat in 2036, (2) it earns ~20% ROIC in perpetuity, (3) the WACC stays ~9%. What does the terminal value assume about competitive position? **That Netflix in 2036 remains the global leader of a rational streaming oligopoly with a scaled ad business — essentially, the market position it holds today.** That is a demanding but not unreasonable assumption; it is precisely what a DCF _should_ price.

# 13. Reverse DCF — What Does the $78 Price Assume?

At $78.16: equity value ≈ $325B, + $7.5B net debt → **required EV ≈ $333B**. My base case EV is $269B — the market is asking for **~24% more value than my base**.

Holding my base growth path and tax rate constant, the price of $78 is consistent with:

- **an 8% WACC** (vs. my 9.0%) — i.e., beta ~0.85, lower than a fair risk estimate for a stock that just fell 38%; **or**
- **~13.5% initial revenue growth** (vs. my 10%) with the same decay; **or**
- **~40–42% terminal margins** (vs. my 36.5%); **or** some combination.

> **At $78, the market is implicitly assuming approximately: either a ~8% cost of capital, or ~13–14% near-term growth, or ~40% terminal margins.** None of these is impossible — each individually is plausible, and the bull case delivers all three — but the market is paying for an outcome between my base case and my bull case, with little margin for disappointment.

That is the whole investment conclusion in one sentence.

# 17. Market Expectations vs. Fundamental Reality

- **What investors are paying:** $78.16 → 24.6x TTM earnings, 22.6x forward, 6.9x sales, ~29x FCF, ~3.4% FCF yield.
- **What must happen for that to be justified:** ~8% WACC or ~13.5% initial growth or ~40%+ terminal margins (Section 13).
- **What I believe happens:** ~9% WACC, ~10% decaying growth, ~36.5% terminal margins → **$63**.

**Price > Value — the market is somewhat too optimistic**, though this is a _much_ milder statement than it was at $127, where the embedded assumptions approached my extreme bull case. The market has already corrected ~38%; my analysis says the correction has not yet gone far enough to create a margin of safety. The market's de-rating has been rational — the question of "how much growth is left" is real — and the current price sits awkwardly between base and bull.

# 18. Real Options & Optionality

Netflix holds genuine, un-priced-in-theory optionality that I deliberately **do not** add on top of the DCF:

1. **Advertising:** the option to monetize ~300M+ engaged subscribers. 2026 ads ~$3B; a bull path to $15–20B by 2035 with 60–80% incremental margins would be worth $100+/share. The base case already credits a large fraction of this; the bull case credits more.
2. **Live sports & events:** NFL, MLB, WWE, boxing, FIFA WC — optionality on acquisition, engagement and ad CPMs. Live programming is ~5% of content spend but drives 6 of the top-10 new-signup days of the past five years. ([SEC][3])
3. **Games:** cloud gaming (FIFA, Unhinged, GTA VI marketing moment) is a small but real long-dated option; some studios have been closed (Night School, Moonloot), signaling discipline. ([StockAnalysis][4])
4. **Licensing/aggregation:** the TF1 partnership and The Walking Dead $500M deal show Netflix can aggregate third-party content on favorable terms. ([SEC][3])
5. **The abandonment option:** content spend is largely discretionary year-to-year — Netflix can cut spend ~20% in a downturn and convert revenue into cash (it did exactly this in 2022–23).

The correct treatment (and mine): the scenario ranges — not an arbitrary option premium — monetize these options.

# 19. Special Valuation Situations

Netflix is a mature platform/network business requiring a **two-stage FCFF model** (which I use). FCFF (not FCFE) is the right cash flow given the modest but real debt layer and the buyback program; the discount rate is WACC (not cost of equity), consistently applied. Two special considerations: (1) **content assets are operating capital, not working capital** — ROIC must be presented both including and excluding them (17% vs 32%); (2) employee options (~124M shares, ~3% of shares) are handled via the diluted share count rather than an option-pricing overlay.

# 20. Valuation Error & Uncertainty

**Confidence: Medium.** Estimation uncertainty is moderate (margins, ad ramp, growth decay); economic uncertainty is moderate (streaming is settled but ads are not); information uncertainty is low (excellent disclosure); model uncertainty is moderate (is Netflix a media company, a tech platform, or a consumer subscription? The model choice changes the multiple, but the FCFF identity holds regardless). I think of intrinsic value as **~$50–85** with meaningful tails — not a point estimate of $63. The direction of the error bar at $78 is what matters: the _central_ case is below the price.

# 21. Inversion: What Would Make This Investment Fail?

Three assumptions whose failure would most damage the thesis:

1. **Margin trajectory reverses.** Content amortization (+10% in 2026) accelerates into a content arms race (sports rights, AI-era talent costs), ads disappoint, and operating margin stalls below 31% — the DCF falls toward $45–50.
2. **The discount rate stays high.** If the market keeps treating Netflix like a 1.5-beta volatile growth stock (WACC ~10%) instead of a mature compounder (9%), fair value is ~$53 even on base fundamentals — i.e., $78 remains ~45% above fair value indefinitely.
3. **Capital-allocation recidivism.** A second WBD-style mega-deal that actually closes (debt-financed legacy media or gaming) could transform a $10B FCF compounder into a leveraged conglomerate — the failure mode that most worries me after December 2025.

Other monitored risks: ad-tier cannibalization of premium ARPU, a cold content slate (engagement already only +2%), regulation (EU levies, Brazil taxes), and FX on 57% of revenue.

# The Five Most Important Conclusions

## 1. The Business

**Netflix is a mature, dominant global subscription-entertainment franchise with an emerging advertising layer** — ~$48B revenue, ~30% operating margins, ~$10B normalized FCF, ~300M+ subscribers, and an audience approaching 1B people. It is no longer a hyper-growth story; it is a cash-compounding story.

## 2. The Economics

The single most important driver of intrinsic value is **terminal operating margin and its durability** — whether Netflix sustains ~35%+ margins while content costs stay controlled. ROIC (17–32%) already far exceeds WACC (9%), so growth is unambiguously value-creating; the margin path determines _how much_ value.

## 3. The Market's Expectations

At $78, the market is pricing **roughly an 8% cost of capital, or ~13–14% near-term growth, or ~40% terminal margins** — a plausible but optimistic combination that sits between my base case ($63) and bull case ($96). The 38% de-rating of the past year was a rational partial reset, not a capitulation.

## 4. The Valuation

My central DCF is **~$63/share** (9.0% WACC, 36.5% terminal margin, 3% terminal growth), and probability-weighted value is **~$70**. The valuation rests heavily on the terminal value (63% of EV), which in turn rests on the moat persisting — an assumption I believe, but which the price at $78 leaves no room to be wrong about.

## 5. The Investment Decision

At ~$78, **hold/wait — do not buy**. This is a wonderful business trading ~24% above my base case and ~10% above weighted value, with a negative expected return over my horizon. I would become a buyer around **$50–60**, where the margin of safety flips positive and the expected return compensates for the genuine uncertainty around margins, ads and the discount rate.

> **"At a price of roughly $78, the market is effectively assuming that Netflix can be valued like a low-risk, mature compounder — an ~8% cost of capital, double-digit growth for several more years, and ~40% operating margins at the terminal point — or equivalently that the advertising business will scale to tens of billions with media-grade margins. My valuation implies approximately $63/share in the base case and ~$70 on a probability-weighted basis because I expect guided margin expansion and ads growth to continue, but require a 9% cost of capital for a stock whose regression beta is 1.5 and whose subscriber growth is largely complete. The biggest risks to this valuation are a reversal of the margin trajectory, a persistently high discount rate, and capital-allocation recidivism after the WBD episode. Therefore, at today's price, I would hold or wait rather than buy, and I would become substantially more interested around $50–60, because only there does the margin of safety begin to compensate for the uncertainty."**

### The single most important thing to remember

**Do not watch Netflix's subscriber count. Watch its operating margin and its ads revenue.** If margins keep grinding toward ~35%+ and ads scale past $3B toward $10B, my valuation should rise. If margin expansion stalls while content amortization keeps growing 10% annually, the correct Damodaran conclusion is that the market was right to de-rate the stock — and $78 is not yet cheap.

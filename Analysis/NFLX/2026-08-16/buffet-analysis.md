# NFLX Value Investment Analysis Report - Warrent Buffett Lens

# Netflix, Inc. (NASDAQ: NFLX) — Buffett-Style Value Investment Analysis

**Bottom line first:** Netflix is, today, what Buffett's framework asks most businesses to become and almost none ever do: a **dominant consumer franchise that has stopped consuming capital and started spewing it out**. In 2021 it burned cash; in 2026 it is on track to generate roughly **$12.5B of free cash flow** on **~$51B of revenue** with **~31.5% operating margins**, buying back stock by the billions while competitors bleed. The moat — global scale amortized over 325M+ memberships, a brand built over two decades, and a content flywheel that turned the former contenders into _licensors to Netflix_ — has been tested by Disney, Warner, Apple, Amazon, and YouTube, and it has held.

The only thing missing from the Buffett checklist is the one Buffett himself says matters most: **price**.

At **$78.16** (August 14, 2026 close) and a **$325B market cap**, Netflix trades at roughly **29–30× normalized owner earnings** and **29× TTM free cash flow**. That is _fair-to-fully valued_ for a wonderful business — not a bargain, and certainly not the 30–50% margin of safety Buffett requires before he pulls the trigger.

My conclusion using a strict **Buffett-style framework** is:

> **Rating: B — Buy (quality is A-grade; today's price is C-grade)**
>
> **A wonderful business at a fair price — but not a bargain price.**
>
> I would become an eager buyer around **$52–64** (15–30% below my base intrinsic value of ~$70), and I would regard **<$52 as genuinely compelling** if the fundamentals remain intact. Below **$45**, the stock would be approaching a 50% discount to base-case value — classic Buffett territory.
>
> Above **$92**, the market is paying for the bull case, and a Buffett disciple would be a seller, not a buyer.
>
> If you already own Netflix, hold it. It is one of the best businesses on Earth. But new money demands patience: **price is what you pay; value is what you get.**

The distinction matters enormously: this is not a "C/Watch" like IREN, where the business itself is unproven. This is a **"B" where the business is nearly flawless and only the entry price is imperfect**.

# 2. First: What Exactly Is Netflix?

This part is blessedly simple — which is exactly why Buffett would like it.

> **Netflix charges ~325M+ households around the world a monthly subscription to watch movies, TV series, games, and live events. It spends money on content, and it keeps what's left.**

It is a one-segment business. Revenues are "primarily derived from monthly membership fees," and the company manages itself to a single number: **operating margin**, with **revenue growth** as the growth metric. ([SEC][8])

The details that matter for a Buffett lens:

- **FY2025**: revenue **$45.18B** (+16%), operating income **$13.33B** (29.5% margin, up from 26.7%), net income **$10.98B**, operating cash flow **$10.15B**. ([SEC][8])
- **Q2 2026** (reported July 16, 2026): revenue **$12.56B** (+13.4%), operating income **$4.19B** (33.4% margin), net income **$3.40B**, diluted EPS **$0.80**. ([SEC][9])
- **2026 guidance**: revenue **$51.0–51.4B** (+13–14%), operating margin **~31.5%**, **~$12.5B of FCF**, ads revenue roughly doubling to **~$3B**. ([SEC][9])
- More than **325M paid memberships** at end-2025, serving an audience "approaching 1B people." ([SEC][10])

That's the whole business model. Any ordinary person can understand it, and the SEC filings are refreshingly free of the acronym-soup that plagues so many companies.

# 3. Circle of Competence

## Is the business understandable?

**Yes — almost completely.**

- **How it makes money:** memberships (plus a fast-growing ad tier and minor consumer products/live experiences). Billing is monthly, in advance; deferred revenue is tiny (~$1.8B, mostly one month of billed fees). ([SEC][8])
- **What it spends on:** content amortization (~36% of revenue in 2025), marketing (~7%), technology (~8%), G&A (~4%). ([SEC][8])
- **What determines success:** does the content keep people subscribed? Do churn and acquisition stay balanced? Does pricing power cover content inflation?

This is Coca-Cola, not cryptocurrencies. Buffett's own framing applies directly: entertainment is a **share-of-mind** business, and Netflix has spent 25+ years winning share of mind.

## Will the product still be needed in 10 years?

**Almost certainly yes.** People will pay for on-demand video entertainment in 2036 the way they pay for it today — the only question is **who** they pay. Netflix is the default answer in most of the world.

## Will technology fundamentally change this industry?

**Possibly — and this is the honest risk.** Generative AI is lowering the cost of producing video and enabling YouTube/TikTok-style creator platforms to flood the zone. Netflix itself now uses GenAI workflows in **~300 of its 2026 titles**, mostly in post-production. ([SEC][9]) If AI makes premium video abundant and cheap, the scarcity value of Netflix's library could compress — or Netflix, with the best data and distribution, could be the biggest beneficiary of cheaper production. This cuts both ways, but it is not the same kind of existential obsolescence risk IREN faces with GPUs.

### Circle-of-competence score: **8/10**

I understand this business, its unit economics, and its competitive dynamics. What I cannot predict with precision is _creative taste_ at the margin — which is why I insist on a margin of safety in the price.

# 4. Economic Moat Analysis

This is where the market's history lesson is clearest. Disney, Warner, Apple, Amazon, Peacock, Paramount+ — some of the best-capitalized companies on Earth — spent a decade and hundreds of billions trying to kill Netflix. They failed so completely that:

- Disney's streaming segment made **$712M** of operating income in the June 2026 quarter — against Netflix's **$4.19B** of operating income in the _same_ quarter. Disney stopped disclosing Disney+ and Hulu subscriber counts in early 2026 (last disclosed: Disney+ ~132M, ~196M including Hulu). ([CNBC][18], [Newscast][19], [Variety][20])
- Warner Bros. Discovery was acquired by Paramount Skydance (cleared by the UK CMA in August 2026) — consolidation is what happens to losers. ([TipRanks][16])
- Paramount+ reached **81.6M** subscribers — one quarter of Netflix's base — and Paramount just had "its best quarter for retention in Paramount+'s history" _after_ being acquired. ([TipRanks][16])
- Studios that once hoarded content now **license their crown jewels back to Netflix**: The Walking Dead universe went to Netflix in a reported **~$500M** deal (July 2026). ([TechCrunch][25])

When your fiercest competitors conclude that selling you their best content is better economics than competing with you, that is the definition of a moat.

---

## 4.1 Intangible Assets

### Brand

**Extraordinary.** "Netflix" is a verb in a hundred languages. In 2025, members watched **96 billion hours** in the second half alone; H1 2026 hours grew +2% to **97 billion** _despite_ the Winter Olympics and World Cup competing for attention. ([SEC][10][9]) Advertisers just **nearly doubled their upfront commitments** to Netflix in 2026. ([TipRanks][15])

The brand survived — with damage — the 2025 Musk-led boycott campaign (the company's own proxy material notes the stock's drawdown "since Musk's call for cancellations"), which tells you the brand is strong enough to take a hit and keep 325M+ memberships. ([SEC][10])

### Patents / licenses

Not the moat. Netflix owns real technology (recommendation, Open Connect CDN, encoding) but its advantages are replicable in principle.

### Regulatory barriers

Net-neutrality-ish. Actually a _negative_ in some regions: the EU's 30% European-works quota, member-state levies, and content regulations raise costs for everyone, Netflix included. ([SEC][8])

**Score: 9/10 (brand), 3/10 (IP/regulatory)**

---

## 4.2 Switching Costs

**Honestly low.** A member can cancel in 30 seconds, and historically Netflix churns low-single-digits percent of members monthly. There is no data migration, no hardware lock-in, no contract.

The _real_ switching costs are softer: losing access to a decade of favorites, the kids' profiles, and being out of the cultural conversation. These are real but not contractual.

**Score: 4/10** — with the caveat that retention is won by _habit_, not by lock-in.

---

## 4.3 Network Effects

**Partial, and misunderstood by skeptics.** Netflix's network effect is not "more users → more utility for existing users" (that's the phone network). It is:

1. **Scale → content budget → hit rate.** 325M+ memberships fund a content machine no one else can match. H1 2026 alone produced multiple global hits (I Will Find You, Apex, Swapped). ([SEC][9])
2. **Cultural conversation.** Netflix shows dominate water-cooler talk; live events drove **6 of the top 10 new-member sign-up days of the last five years** — and Netflix has only done live events since 2023. ([SEC][9])

Competitors cannot easily replicate the first effect without first achieving Netflix's scale — a circular problem that has defeated them for a decade.

**Score: 6/10** — real, but not Visa/Moody's-grade.

---

## 4.4 Cost Advantage — The Deepest Moat

This is the Buffett-relevant one, and it is powerful:

> **A hit TV show costs roughly the same whether 50M or 500M people watch it. Netflix amortizes that fixed cost over the largest paid audience in streaming history.**

- Content amortization was **$16.42B** in 2025 — spread over **$45.18B** of revenue. ([SEC][8]) Disney cannot spread its content budget over a comparable base profitably; Paramount+ and Max cannot either.
- **Capex is trivial**: only **~$688M** in 2025, under **2% of revenue**. ([SEC][8]) The business requires almost no factories, networks, or machinery — the opposite of IREN.
- **Cash content spend ≈ amortization**: management guides 2026 cash content spend to **~1.1× amortization** — i.e., the business is not quietly deferring a capex bill into the future. ([SEC][9])
- Distribution is proprietary (Open Connect) and effectively free at the margin.

The result: Netflix's **29.7% TTM operating margin** and **~23% FCF margin** while competitors earn pennies (Disney DTC: $712M/quarter on ~$5.5B revenue = ~13% and improving). ([StockAnalysis][4][6], [Newscast][19])

**Score: 9/10**

---

## 4.5 Overall Moat Rating

**Moderate → Wide.** I call it **Wide on economics, Moderate on durability**. The width comes from brand + scale economics that are empirically hard to attack; the "Moderate" discount reflects that switching costs are low and AI could, someday, commoditize premium video supply. A decade of evidence says the moat repels the largest, best-funded incursions in business history.

# 5. Management Quality

## 5.1 Integrity & Shareholder Orientation

- **Candor:** Netflix's shareholder letters are models of plain-spoken honesty. They admit when things go wrong (the 2022 subscriber miss), give precise internal forecasts (not sandbagged ranges), and explain mistakes. In 2025 the company **stopped reporting member counts** — a decision that frustrated the Street but was framed transparently: revenue and operating margin are the metrics management actually runs. ([SEC][8])
- **Governance:** one share/one vote, majority voting, annual elections, proxy access, no staggered board. Say-on-pay passed with **85.3%** support. ([SEC][10])
- **Skin in the game:** Reed Hastings beneficially owns **37.8M shares** (~$2.9B); Sarandos **5.7M**, Peters **2.7M**. All insiders combined: 1.24% of the company. ([SEC][10])
- **Compensation:** this is the sticking point. Sarandos and Peters each took home **~$53M** in 2025 ($3M salary + $41.4M stock awards + $7.05M bonus). Pay ratio is **255:1**. ([SEC][10]) The program is now 50/50 PSU/RSU with PSUs tied to **relative TSR vs. the S&P 500** — better than the old option-heavy design, but $53M/year per co-CEO while shareholders got a 38% drawdown is hard to defend from a Buffett chair. Related-party disclosures (Sarandos' family members working on productions) are disclosed and seem immaterial. ([SEC][10])
- **Insider selling:** routine and steady. August 2026 Form 4s show Sarandos selling ~133k shares, Peters 27k, CFO Neumann 9k — all under 10b5-1 plans. ([SEC][11], [TipRanks][24]) Netflix insiders have sold heavily for two decades; it is a fact of life here, not a new signal.

## 5.2 Capital Allocation — The $83 Billion Story

This is the most Buffett-relevant chapter in Netflix's recent history, and it cuts both ways:

1. **The disciplined part:** Netflix returned **$9.1B** via buybacks in 2025, **$6.2B** in 2024, **$6.0B** in 2023 — **~$21.4B in three years**, funded entirely by internal FCF. In April 2026 the board added a **$25B** authorization; Q2 2026 saw the **largest buyback quarter ever ($4.7B)**, with **$27.1B** of capacity remaining. ([SEC][8][9]) It pays no dividend and won't soon — correct for a business this compoundable.
2. **The alarming part:** in December 2025 Netflix signed an **~$82.7B enterprise value** agreement to acquire Warner Bros. Discovery's streaming and studios businesses — including HBO — financed via a **$42.2B bridge facility**, a $5B revolver and a $20B delayed-draw term loan. ([SEC][8]) For a company whose entire advantage is a clean, focused, asset-light model, swallowing WBD's studios and HBO was an empire-scale gamble. When Paramount Skydance topped the bid in February 2026, Netflix **walked away** — and collected a **$2.8B termination fee** (recorded in Q1 2026 as other income). ([THR][12], [Variety][13], [SEC][9])

Buffett's read: **the walk-away was excellent discipline; the attempt was a yellow flag.** A management team willing to contemplate an $83B, leverage-financed merger of this complexity may not be done trying to get bigger. Watch this tendency closely.

## 5.3 Operational Capability

Nearly unmatched. Twenty years of execution across 190+ countries, a culture that famously retains only high performers, and a product that defines its category. Gross margin expanded from **41.6% (2021)** to **49.1% (TTM)**; operating margin from **20.9% to 29.7%** over the same window. ([StockAnalysis][4])

### Management score: **8/10** — elite operators; docked for comp levels, the WBD flirtation, and routine insider selling.

# 6. Financial Health & Earnings Quality

## 6.1 Profitability — Ten-Year Trend (split-adjusted)

| Metric           |   TTM |  2025 |  2024 |  2023 |  2022 |  2021 |
| ---------------- | ----: | ----: | ----: | ----: | ----: | ----: |
| Revenue ($B)     |  48.4 |  45.2 |  39.0 |  33.7 |  31.6 |  29.7 |
| Operating margin | 29.7% | 29.5% | 26.7% | 20.6% | 17.8% | 20.9% |
| Net margin       | 28.2% | 24.3% | 22.3% | 16.0% | 14.2% | 17.2% |
| Diluted EPS      | $3.17 | $2.53 | $1.98 | $1.20 | $0.99 | $1.12 |
| ROE              | 49.5% | 42.8% | 38.4% | 26.2% | 24.5% | 38.0% |
| ROIC             | 32.4% | 34.0% | 28.7% | 19.5% | 16.1% | 22.0% |

([StockAnalysis][4][7])

This is a **textbook margin-and-returns staircase**. ROE has been >24% for five straight years **without leverage as the driver** — debt/equity fell from 1.14 (2021) to **0.55** (TTM). ROIC of ~32% is several times any reasonable cost of capital. The 2025 TTM EPS includes the $2.8B one-time WBD termination fee (~$0.55/share); ex-fee, TTM EPS is roughly **$2.70–2.75** and normalized ROE is still ~40%+. ([SEC][9], [THR][12])

## 6.2 Cash Flow Quality — The Buffett Test, Passed

**Free cash flow history** (OCF − capex, $B): **−0.13 (2021) → 1.62 (2022) → 6.93 (2023) → 6.92 (2024) → 9.46 (2025) → 11.15 (TTM)**. ([StockAnalysis][6])

- **OCF/NI:** 0.08× (2021) → 0.45× (2022) → 1.35× (2023) → 0.85× (2024) → 0.92× (2025) → 0.88× (TTM). Note the subtlety: Netflix books **content cash spend inside operating cash flow** ("Additions to content assets" was **$17.1B** in 2025), so its OCF is _already net_ of the content bill. That is why OCF ≈ NI here — content is cash-paid. ([SEC][8]) This is conservative, not aggressive, presentation.
- **Capex as % of revenue:** **1.5% (2025)**. Almost no fixed-asset treadmill. ([SEC][8])
- **FCF margin:** **23.1% TTM**; management guides **~$12.5B FCF for 2026** (≈24% margin). ([SEC][9])
- **Stock-based comp:** only **$368M** in 2025 (~0.8% of revenue) — modest for tech. ([SEC][8])
- **Receivables:** ~$2.0B, de minimis risk. No inventory. ([SEC][5])

This is the profile of a toll-booth with a subscription wrapper. Buffett's "does the business require massive ongoing investment?" — the answer is **no**, which is rarer than most investors realize.

## 6.3 Financial Safety

- **Gross debt:** **$14.31B** (senior notes) at 6/30/26; **$14.46B** at end-2025; twelve tranches maturing 2026–2054, fixed-rate, with only **$1.69B** principal+interest due in the next 12 months and **$1B** maturing late 2026 (to be refinanced). ([SEC][9][8])
- **Cash & equivalents:** **$9.10B** (6/30/26). **Net debt: $5.24B** — barely **0.5× TTM EBITDA**. ([SEC][9])
- **Interest coverage:** EBIT **$14.4B** / interest **$0.85B** ≈ **~17×** (TTM). ([StockAnalysis][4])
- **Liquidity:** $3B undrawn revolver (2029) + $3B commercial paper program, both unused. ([SEC][8])
- **Off-balance-sheet:** this is the one to respect — **$25.1B of total streaming content obligations** (only $5.5B of which sits on the balance sheet), plus ~$2.9B of leases. Content is largely a fixed cost; if revenue growth stalls, this is the margin lever that becomes a liability. Also **$566M** of gross unrecognized tax benefits and ~$700M of expected Brazil tax deposits. ([SEC][9][8])

**Debt/equity 0.55 — far below the 50%-ish caution line once you net cash.** No covenant concerns (all notes/credit facilities compliant). ([SEC][8])

## 6.4 Accounting Prudence

- **Accelerated content amortization:** >90% of a title amortizes within four years, front-loaded by expected viewing. EY's one critical audit matter is precisely this estimate — and it's a conservative-looking one. ([SEC][8])
- **No goodwill of consequence, no aggressive revenue recognition** (revenue is ratably recognized monthly subscriptions), no frequent policy changes, clean EY opinions with auditor since 2012. ([SEC][8])
- The 2025 income statement carries a **$619M Brazil non-income tax charge** recognized _as soon as probable_ — a mark of conservative provisioning. ([SEC][8])

### Earnings quality score: **8/10** (one-time WBD fee is the only material noise)

# 7. Long-Term Growth Prospects

## 7.1 Industry Trends

Streaming is now a **stable oligopoly with one clear leader**. The shakeout is finished — the players left standing are Netflix, YouTube (different animal), Disney+, Prime Video (a loss-leader inside Amazon), and a few regional operators. Losers are being consolidated (Paramount-WBD, CMA-cleared August 2026). ([TipRanks][16]) Netflix's letter observes its own H1 view hours grew _faster_ in 2026 (+2%) than 2025 (+1.5%) despite Olympic and World Cup competition — engagement, the real battleground, is holding. ([SEC][9])

## 7.2 Company Competitive Position

- **Share:** the largest paid video membership base in history: **325M+ paying memberships**, audience approaching 1B people. ([SEC][10][9])
- **Pricing power:** proven repeatedly. 2025 revenue growth was driven by "growth in memberships, **price increases**, and increased advertising revenue." H1 2026 price rises in the US, Mexico and Spain landed "consistent with prior price changes and our expectations." Paid plans now range from $1 to **$37/month** equivalent globally — a ~30× price ladder. ([SEC][8][9])
- **The ads second curve:** ad revenue more than 2.5×'d to **>$1.5B in 2025**, guided to **~$3B in 2026**, with the ads tier at **250M monthly active viewers** (May 2026 upfronts, up from 190M in November 2025) and upfront commitments nearly doubled. Ads are expanding to **15 new countries**. ([SEC][10][9], [TheWrap][14], [TipRanks][15]) Context: YouTube does **$11.1B of ad revenue per quarter** — Netflix has perhaps 1/15th of that today, which is why this is called a _second curve_, not a peaked one. ([TheDesk][17])
- **Live sports as acquisition weapon:** WWE (10-year, **~$5B**, ~$550M/yr, with opt-out after 5 years), NFL (expanded 2026 agreement: week-one game, Thanksgiving Eve, Christmas Gameday, plus a Q1'27 game), MLB events, Tyson Fury vs. Anthony Joshua, the Women's World Cup. Live programming is only ~**5% of content spend and ~1% of view hours** — but drove 6 of the top 10 sign-up days in five years. ([Sportico][21], [SEC][9])
- **Games/creator expansion:** cloud games (FIFA World Cup: Launch Edition), video podcasts (iHeartMedia), creator deals (Ms. Rachel, Danny Go!), and a GTA VI gameplay reveal premiering on Netflix August 27, 2026. ([SEC][9], [TipRanks][16])

## 7.3 Sustainable Growth — the Buffett Test

- **Organic, nearly all of it.** Twenty years of growth came from membership, pricing, and now ads — not serial M&A (the one big attempt, WBD, was abandoned).
- **Growth requires almost no incremental capital** — capex is ~1.5% of revenue. Every dollar of incremental FCF either compounds internally or returns to shareholders. This is the single most Buffett-quality trait in the whole thesis.
- **Room to run:** APAC and LATAM ARPU are a fraction of UCAN's; ads are 1/15th of YouTube's; paid sharing continues to convert password-sharers.

### Growth score: **8/10** — 13–14% near-term with margin expansion is excellent, but the law of large numbers eventually bites.

# 8. Intrinsic Value & Margin of Safety

## 8.1 Discounted Cash Flow — Three Scenarios

I model **normalized owner FCF**, starting from management's ~$12.5B 2026 guide, 10-year horizon, 3% terminal growth (no higher than GDP).

| Scenario  | FCF growth (yrs 1–5 / 6–10) | Discount | Equity value | **Value/share** |
| --------- | --------------------------: | -------: | -----------: | --------------: |
| Bear      |                     7% / 7% |      10% |       ~$226B |        **~$54** |
| Base      |                    12% / 8% |      10% |       ~$277B |        **~$66** |
| Base (9%) |                    12% / 8% |       9% |       ~$298B |        **~$72** |
| Bull      |                   15% / 10% |       9% |       ~$387B |        **~$93** |

(Net debt of $5.2B subtracted; ~4.16B diluted shares.) ([SEC][9], [StockAnalysis][3])

**Base case ≈ $70/share.** Current price of **$78** is ~11% _above_ base case — the market is already paying for modest execution of the base-to-bull path.

## 8.2 Buffett's Owner Earnings

> Owner Earnings = Net Income + D&A − CapEx − ΔWC

TTM: $13.65B NI + $0.37B D&A − $0.82B capex − (−$0.88B WC release) ≈ **$14.1B — but this includes the $2.8B one-time WBD fee**. Normalized (ex-fee, neutral working capital): **≈ $10.7B**. ([StockAnalysis][4][5][6])

At $325B market cap, that is **~30× owner earnings** (3.3% yield). A 25× multiple (still generous for a mature franchise) implies **$64/share**; 30× implies **$77/share**. Owner earnings do **not** support today's price as a bargain. ([StockAnalysis][3][7])

## 8.3 Relative Valuation

- **P/E 24.6** (reported TTM) / **~29** normalized — versus **36–44×** at each year-end 2023–2025 and **52×** in 2021. Today's multiple is at the **low end of its own five-year range**. ([StockAnalysis][7])
- **Forward P/E 22.6**; **PEG 1.13**. ([StockAnalysis][3])
- **EV/EBITDA 22.6** vs. 29.7 (2025) and 36.3 (2024). ([StockAnalysis][7])
- **P/FCF 29.2** — FCF yield **3.4%** and rising fast (~$12.5B FCF in 2026 = ~3.8% on today's cap). ([StockAnalysis][7][9])
- The stock has already fallen **38% from its 52-week high of $126.71** (low: $65.08) — the market has done some of the discounting work. Ackman's Pershing Square just opened a **3.15M-share** position declaring "Netflix has effectively won the streaming wars." ([StockAnalysis][3], [TipRanks][22], [Investopedia][23])

## 8.4 Margin of Safety

At $78 vs. ~$70 base intrinsic value: **no margin of safety** — in fact a ~11% premium.

- 30% discount to base = **~$49**; 50% discount = **~$35**.
- If bear-case $54 is right, today's price overpays ~45%.
- If bull-case $93 is right, today's price offers ~19% upside.

Buffett doesn't buy expected values; he buys **high-probability discounts**. Today's price offers a high-quality company at a fair price — good for holders, insufficient for disciplined new buyers.

### Valuation score: **5/10** | Margin of safety score: **4/10**

# 9. Major Risks

## Risk #1 — Competition for attention (YouTube, TikTok, AI-generated video)

YouTube's ad revenue alone is ~**$11.1B/quarter** — an entertainment machine with 2.5B users and zero content cost, plus now live sports. ([TheDesk][17]) TikTok/short-form video and AI-lowered production costs compete for the same 24 hours. Netflix's own 10-K lists piracy as a top competitor with "virtually all content for free." ([SEC][8])

## Risk #2 — Content cost & sports-rights inflation

WWE cost ~$550M/year; the NFL expansion isn't free; boxing and MLB add up. Live rights have historically inflated faster than subscription pricing. Live is still only ~5% of content spend — but the direction of travel matters. ([Sportico][21], [SEC][9])

## Risk #3 — Labor disruptions & creative dependence

The WGA agreement expires **May 1, 2026**; SAG-AFTRA and DGA on **June 30, 2026** — right now. The 2023 strikes froze the pipeline and compressed margins; a repeat is a real, near-dated risk. Also: a business whose product is hits is hostage to taste, and hits are not forecastable. ([SEC][8])

## Risk #4 — Management ambition & governance

The **$83B WBD merger attempt** (December 2025) proved management will contemplate empire-scale, leverage-financed deals. The company that walked away once might not walk away twice. Combined with ~$53M/year co-CEO comp and routine insider selling, this is the main self-inflicted risk. ([SEC][8][10], [THR][12])

## Risk #5 — Valuation & growth deceleration

At ~29× normalized earnings, the price embeds continued double-digit growth. Netflix's own trajectory shows deceleration is inevitable (17.6% revenue growth in Q4'25 → 13.4% in Q2'26 → ~11.7% guided Q3'26). If growth slips to high-single digits while the multiple mean-reverts to ~20×, the stock would have ~30–40% downside from here. ([SEC][9], [StockAnalysis][7])

## Risk #6 — Regulatory, tax & FX

EU content quotas/levies, Brazil's $619M non-income tax precedent, and **56% of revenue denominated in non-USD currencies** (constant-currency revenue was ~$271M higher than reported in 2025). ([SEC][8])

## Risk #7 — Key person

Sarandos and Peters are the franchise; Hastings has now fully departed the board (2026 annual meeting). Succession has been smooth so far, but creative leadership is not a commodity. ([SEC][10])

# 10. Investment Decision Framework

**Buy conditions (all must be met):**

- ✅ Business simple and within circle of competence
- ✅ Wide/sustainable moat (proven for a decade against the best-funded attacks in history)
- ✅ Integrity + strong capital allocation (buybacks, walk-away discipline — with the WBD caveat)
- ✅ Financial soundness, ample and growing FCF (~$12.5B guided 2026)
- ✅ Clear long-term growth (pricing, ads, international)
- ❌ **Sufficient margin of safety — FAILS at $78** (needs ≥30% discount to ~$70 base value)
- ✅ Risks understandable (though competition risk is genuinely dynamic)

**Hold conditions:** fundamentals intact (they are); moat intact (it is); no integrity failure (none); not severely overvalued (it isn't — yet).

**Sell conditions:**

1. Moat damage: sustained engagement declines (view hours are the new scoreboard) or studios re-monetizing content against Netflix en masse
2. Another empire-scale M&A attempt that _succeeds_
3. Severe overvaluation: stock above **~$105–110** (>50% above base intrinsic value)
4. Management integrity failure or creative-leadership disruption
5. Growth decelerates below ~8% with margin flat — thesis broken at 29× earnings

# 12. What I Like Most About Netflix

1. **A toll booth with no toll road to maintain** — ~1.5% capex/revenue, ~23–24% FCF margins, ~$12.5B of guided 2026 FCF.
2. **The moat has already won its war** — competitors now license Netflix their best content.
3. **Proven pricing power** with a $1–37/month global ladder and price increases that land.
4. **Two genuine second curves** — ads (~$3B and doubling, 250M MAU) and live events — plus games/podcasts as options.
5. **Shareholder-friendly capital returns** — $21B+ of buybacks in three years, $27B of remaining authorization, no wasteful dividends or dilutive M&A (so far).

# 13. What I Like Least

1. **The price.** ~29–30× owner earnings is a fair price for a wonderful company — and Buffett's whole system exists to avoid paying fair prices.
2. **The WBD episode.** An $83B leverage-financed merger attempt reveals a management capable of empire-building; the discipline was in the walk-away, not the attempt.
3. **Executive compensation** — ~$53M/year per co-CEO (255:1 ratio) is far beyond the "skin in the game" Buffett would tolerate.
4. **Creative risk** — hits are not a manufacturing process; a two-year cold streak would show in churn and margins.
5. **No margin of safety cushion** — a 2026 guild strike or a growth air-pocket would be painful at this multiple.

# 14. The Most Important Metric Going Forward

Forget the subscriber count (Netflix stopped reporting it — correctly). The Buffett metric is:

## **Free cash flow per diluted share after content cash spend**

Track: FCF/share ($2.59 TTM → ~$2.95 guided 2026), content cash spend vs. amortization (~1.1×), buyback yield, and view-hours growth as the engagement scoreboard. If FCF/share compounds mid-teens while the multiple stays put, the stock compounds in lockstep. ([SEC][9], [StockAnalysis][6])

# 16. Final Investment Judgment

## **NFLX = B / BUY — a wonderful company at a fair price**

Netflix passes nearly every Buffett test with honors: it is simple, dominant, asset-light, cash-generative, honestly run, and led by operators with a quarter-century track record. Its economics — 29.7% operating margins, 23% FCF margins, 32% ROIC, capex under 2% of revenue — would make See's Candies blush. This is the kind of business Buffett describes when he says the ideal holding is one you'd be happy to own if the stock market closed for ten years.

The failure is singular and classical: **price**. At ~$78, Netflix trades at ~29–30× normalized owner earnings and ~11% above my base-case intrinsic value of ~$70. There is no margin of safety. A disciplined Buffett-style buyer therefore:

- **Holds** if already an owner (fundamentals are intact and the stock is not severely overvalued);
- **Waits for $64 and starts buying;**
- **Backs up the truck below $52–55;**
- **Avoids or trims above $92.**

The most important Buffett reminder for this name: the 38% drawdown from $126.71 to the $65–78 range has converted a _ridiculous_ valuation into merely a _full_ one. The gap between "a wonderful company" and "a wonderful investment" is always closed by price — and for Netflix, that gap is now only one more bad quarter wide. Patience will be rewarded; haste will not. ([StockAnalysis][3])

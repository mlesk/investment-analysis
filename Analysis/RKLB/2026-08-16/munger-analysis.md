# Charlie Munger Lens — Rocket Lab Corporation (RKLB)

**Date:** 2026-08-16 · **Reference price:** $80.25 (close 8/14/26) · **Market cap:** ~$48.0B

---

## Bottom-Line Verdict

**Rating: D — Avoid.** This is a stock priced for a flawless 10-year execution of the most difficult program in the industry, sold by a company that is burning ~$370M/yr of free cash flow, diluting shareholders ~16% per year, paying no dividend, generating no GAAP earnings, and — as I write this — about to lever up its balance sheet and issue a large block of stock to buy a profitable satellite operator for $8B. The business may one day be wonderful; I cannot know that, and neither can the market. What I can say with confidence is that the price does not give me, a sober man, any safety. My conservative DCF — a 21.7% discount rate (WACC +3%) and terminal growth of 0–2% — values this stock at **$3.44–$8.38 per share** against a market price of **$80.25**. Even in my bull case the stock is worth less than a tenth of its price. The market is not pricing the business; it is pricing a lottery ticket on Neutron and Iridium. That is a fine thing to watch and a poor thing to buy.

There is real substance here: Peter Beck has built the West's leading small-lift rocket, a private launch pad in New Zealand, 87 successful missions, a 100%-success 2026 so far, and a $2.36B backlog [SEC][1]. I respect the operator. But "respect the operator" is not a substitute for "the price is fair." A wonderful company at a stupid price is still a stupid investment.

## 1. Circle of Competence Assessment

Munger's test: can I explain this business simply, and can I predict its competitive landscape 10 years out?

I can explain it simply enough — Rocket Lab builds rockets, launches small satellites, and increasingly builds satellites and components for other people. The trouble is the second question. This is a business where the dominant competitor (SpaceX) launches at a scale and price point that has **driven industry-wide launch-price deflation for a decade** [SEC][2], where the flagship growth engine (Neutron) has not yet flown, where a qualification test of the Stage 1 tank **ruptured in January 2026** and pushed first launch to a "narrowing" end-of-2026 window [SEC][2][1], and where the defining strategic bet (Iridium) is a merger of two very different businesses — a project-based launch/satellite house and a subscription telecom — that has never been attempted by this management team.

Can I predict who wins this market in 2035? No. Neither can management, which is why the 10-K is a library of "may not achieve," "risk and uncertainty remains," and "no assurance" [SEC][2]. My honest answer, in Munger's terms: **this is at the edge of my circle, shading into outside.** I understand the words in the financial statements; I do not understand, with any confidence, the physics, schedule, and competitive trajectory that will determine whether the cash flows I must forecast actually arrive.

**Most fragile assumption:** that Neutron reaches a profitable, high-cadence production cadence and that the FCF turns durably positive inside my forecast window. Every dollar of the $48B market cap is resting on that assumption.

**Rating: Outside / Edge** (leaning outside for a disciplined value investor).

## 2. Multidisciplinary Mental Models

### Model 1 — Inversion ("Invert, always invert")

I asked the reverse question first: _what destroys this investment?_ The answer is short and mechanical. (a) Neutron slips again or fails on first flight — the entire medium-lift story collapses and R&D at 45% of revenue keeps burning with no payoff [SEC][2][1]. (b) The Iridium integration fails — an $8.0B EV deal requiring **> $3.0B of cash** plus ~$1.8B to repay/refinance Iridium's term loan, funded by a $3.6B bridge, with two incompatible operating cadences [SEC][1]. (c) The dilution treadmill — the company funds losses by selling stock, so if the price falls the dilution accelerates; it is already +15.7% shares YoY [5]. Every one of these is plausible, and all three compound. Inversion says: this is not a low-probability-of-ruin investment; it is a portfolio of correlated tail risks wearing a growth-stock costume.

### Model 2 — Critical mass / economies of scale

Launch economics are a scale game: fixed costs (pads, factories, engines) must be absorbed by flight rate. Electron reached its niche critical mass — revenue per launch $9.1M vs. cost per launch $4.4M in Q2'26 — and the segment is modestly profitable [SEC][1]. But Electron is small-lift (≤300 kg). The addressable growth is in medium/heavy, where SpaceX already has the lowest cost per kg in history. Neutron must be produced in quantity (the "1-3-5" cadence) before its $50–55M ASP turns profitable [5]. The company has not yet proven it can absorb that fixed cost at the rates it promises; the Stage-1 tank rupture shows how fragile the path is [SEC][2].

### Model 3 — Incentives (compensation psychology)

The operator's incentives are, on net, aligned with _long-term_ value: Beck forfeited 392,155 unvested RSUs in March 2026, redirecting that comp to R&D, and took only a $6.8M package in 2025 (no bonus, no raise) [SEC][1][3]. Good. But the _shareholder's_ incentive math is poisoned by structure: SBC was $71.1M in FY25 and ~$81.6M TTM — about 10.6% of revenue [5][SEC][2] — the 2021 equity plan is an evergreen that **auto-increases by 5% of outstanding shares every January 1** through 2031 [SEC][3], and the CFO's 10b5-1 plan covers 2.52M shares and the COO holds ~893,750 unvested RSUs (~$62M at 12/31/25) [SEC][3]. Management's incentive to sell stock to fund the company is structurally aligned with _their_ compensation and misaligned with the per-share value of minority holders. When a CFO can realize $86M on option exercises in a single year while the company reports a $198M loss, the incentive picture is not a clean one [SEC][3][2].

### Model 4 — Opportunity cost

Munger's rule: the best way to hold one idea is to compare it with the alternatives. At 62x TTM sales, no earnings, no dividend, and a 21.7%-discount-rate intrinsic value under $9, RKLB must be compared with any number of profitable, cash-generating compounders that cost less than this. The expected 5-year return I compute is **negative (−11.7%)**. Even if my scenario probabilities are too pessimistic, the upside needs to be enormous and nearly certain to clear a hurdle that the price has already set at "flawless." The opportunity cost of parking capital here is the entire rest of the market.

### Model 5 — Ecological niche / survival of the fittest

Rocket Lab's honest competitive niche today: the small-satellite dedicated-launch slot that SpaceX does not serve economically, plus a vertically integrated spacecraft-components business. That is a real, defensible ecological niche — the private Mahia pad and the U.S.–New Zealand treaty are genuine, hard-to-replicate assets [SEC][2]. But niches in biology get invaded. SpaceX's Starship cadence, Firefly, Blue Origin, and new entrants all sit at the borders [SEC][2]. The question is not whether the niche exists today — it does — but whether a medium-lift entrant can survive a fight with a larger predator that has a 10-year head start on cost. Survival-of-the-fittest favors the incumbent with scale, not the challenger, no matter how brilliant.

## 3. Inversion — How This Investment Fails

### Scenario A: Neutron slips or fails (probability-weighted the largest threat)

- Stage-1 tank ruptured in a qualification test on **Jan 21, 2026**; first launch pushed to a **"narrowing" end-of-2026 window** [SEC][2][1]. Launch vehicle development is a graveyard of schedule slips; the 10-K itself says "risk and uncertainty remains in the complex development cycle" [SEC][1].
- If Neutron is late into 2027 or fails on flight one, the medium-lift thesis — and the constellation-deployment growth story — does not simply delay, it dies. R&D was $270.7M in FY25 (45% of revenue) and $82.4M in Q2'26 alone [SEC][2][1]. A failure converts years of that spend into a stranded cost and re-rates the stock violently.

### Scenario B: Iridium integration fails

- Iridium is an $8.0B EV deal: $54/share, $27 cash + stock (0.40x if RKLB ≤$67.50; $27/VWAP if between; 0.24x if ≥$112.50) [SEC][1]. It requires **> $3.0B cash** for share consideration, Iridium debt repayment, and fees, plus **~$1.8B** to repay or refinance Iridium's term loan, financed by a **$3.6B committed bridge** [SEC][1].
- This is a project-based, loss-making, high-growth aerospace company levering up to absorb a mature, profitable, subscription telecom with a 66-satellite L-band constellation [5]. Integration of "different business models, management styles, risk tolerances, compensation structures and operating cadences" is, in the company's own risk section, "complex, costly and time-consuming" [SEC][1]. If it fails, the combined company carries billions in debt for a deal that produced no synergy — the classic overreach that Munger warns destroys capital.

### Scenario C: The dilution treadmill

- Shares grew **+15.7% YoY** to 598.5M, and the Q3'26 guide implies a weighted average of ~641M [5][SEC][1].
- FY25: $1.12B net raised via ATM (30.8M shares) + 38.9M shares issued on convertible conversion [SEC][2]. H1'26: $1.51B net ATM + collared forwards on 7.45M shares ($474–642M) + 27.8M shares on conversion [SEC][1]. Additional paid-in capital went from $1.2B (end-2024) to **$4.6B** (mid-2026) [SEC][1][2].
- A company that funds operations by selling equity at whatever price the market offers has a built-in feedback loop: falling price → more shares needed → more supply → falling price. With the ATM authorization now up to **$3.0B** [SEC][1], the supply overhang is enormous and structural.

### Scenario D: Valuation compression (the "everyone else" failure)

- 52-week range $37.57–$151.00; the stock is **−46.9% off its high** already [5]. At 62x sales with no profits, the stock does not need a bad outcome to fall — it needs only _less-than-perfect_ news to re-rate. Beta 2.63 [5]. When a growth multiple compresses, the arithmetic is unforgiving.

### Most fragile of my own assumptions

That the FCF turns positive at all within a decade. My DCF assumes it does (in base/bull). If it does not — if RKLB becomes a perpetual equity-funded burn — then the intrinsic value converges to the net cash, ~$3.77/share [5], and today's price is a 20x markup on hope.

## 5. Earnings-Quality Tests

**Test 1 — Operating cash flow vs. net income.** TTM OCF is **−$222M** while TTM net loss is **−$165M** [5]. Cash burn _exceeds_ the accounting loss by ~$57M — the "quality ratio" is inverted (OCF/NI = 1.34x in the wrong direction). A company whose cash losses are larger than its reported losses is not yet earning anything resembling owner earnings. **Fail.**

**Test 2 — Receivables / working capital vs. revenue.** AR grew from $39.0M (12/31/25) to $112.9M (6/30/26), **+189%** against revenue growth of ~62%; contract assets +53%; inventories +69% (build-ahead for constellations) [SEC][1]. H1'26 working capital consumed ~$136M of cash [SEC][1]. This is inventory/receivable-heavy growth — the opposite of a cash machine. **Fail.**

**Test 3 — CapEx: maintenance vs. expansion.** Capex is $148.7M TTM and $156.3M FY25 [5][SEC][2], almost entirely expansion (Neutron pad LC-3, production, Stennis test complex) — justified _if_ Neutron works, and a stranded cost if it does not. The 10-K flags that additional delays "may require more… capital expenditures than we currently anticipate" [SEC][2]. **Risky expansion capex, not benign maintenance.**

**Test 4 — Goodwill / intangibles.** Goodwill $299M + intangibles $320M ≈ 15% of $4.19B assets [SEC][1] — under the 30% red flag, but growing fast with each acquisition (goodwill up from $205.8M to $299.1M in six months) [SEC][1]. No impairments yet; quality of these assets unproven. **Monitor, not yet failing.**

**Test 5 — SBC.** TTM SBC $81.6M = **~10.6% of revenue**; FY25 $71.1M [5][SEC][2]. Unrecognized comp: ~$191M in performance RSUs plus $108.8M in RSUs (12/31/25) [SEC][1][3] — meaningfully more dilution is already on the books. The 2021 Plan auto-adds 5% of shares outstanding each year through 2031 [SEC][3]. **Fail — SBC is a material, structural dilution of the shareholder base.**

**Test 6 — Owner earnings.** Owner Earnings ≈ NI + D&A − CapEx − ΔWorking Capital. TTM: −$165M + ~$90M (D&A) − $149M (capex) − ~$200M (WC drain) ≈ **−$424M** [5][SEC][1]. Deeply negative. The company creates no owner earnings today; it consumes capital.

**Financial safety margin.** Debt is tiny today ($13.1M converts + $1.7M borrowings) and net cash is $2.25B [SEC][1][5]. _But_ the $3.6B bridge and $1.8B refinancing commitment for Iridium will, if closed, flip the balance sheet from fortress to leveraged overnight [SEC][1]. The safety margin is real today and conditional tomorrow.

**Reading:** At a discount rate that compensates for a beta-2.63, loss-making, diluting, single-project-risk company, the discounted cash flows cannot reach even $9 a share — and the market trades at $80.25, **~9.6x the bull case**. The market is using a discount rate far below the company's own risk profile, or is pricing outcomes beyond my generous bull path, or both. There is no margin of safety here; there is a margin of _hope_.

Munger's rule — "never chase bubbles, P/E >40x" — is exceeded here by an order of magnitude when any earnings-based metric is used.

## 8. Risks

1. **Neutron execution risk (highest).** Stage-1 tank qualification failure Jan 2026; first-launch window "narrowing" for end-2026 [SEC][2][1]. A slip or first-flight failure kills the medium-lift thesis and strands ~$270M/yr R&D.
2. **Iridium integration & leverage risk.** $8.0B EV deal; >$3.0B cash + ~$1.8B debt refinancing; $3.6B bridge; two incompatible operating cadences. Regulatory/FCC approval uncertainty; termination risk [SEC][1].
3. **Structural dilution.** +15.7% shares YoY; $3.0B ATM authorization; evergreen equity plan (+5%/yr); unrecognized SBC ~$300M [5][SEC][1][3].
4. **Negative FCF / equity-funded burn.** TTM FCF −$371M; OCF worse than net loss; working capital consuming cash [5][SEC][1].
5. **Valuation & momentum risk.** 62x sales, beta 2.63, −47% off 52-wk high, 8.2% short interest [5]; any disappointment re-rates violently.
6. **Customer concentration.** Top-5 customers ≈ 49% of FY25 revenue and 77% of backlog; US government ≈ 47% of revenue; single contract (MDA, 11% of AR) [SEC][2][1]. Government shutdowns have already delayed awards and payments [SEC][2].
7. **Key-person risk.** The 10-K explicitly says the company is "highly dependent on the services of Sir Peter Beck," the source of "most of the ideas and execution" — with no key-person insurance [SEC][2].

## 9. Final Rating

Munger's framework requires, for a **Buy**: within circle of competence, wide durable moat, honest and shareholder-oriented management, ROIC >15% with low capital needs, financial health and abundant cash flow, pricing power, fair valuation with 30–40% margin of safety, and a clean inversion pass.

Rocket Lab clears almost none of these _at this price_:

- Circle of competence: **outside/edge** ✗
- Moat: **narrow, unproven at the point of growth** ✗
- ROIC / capital needs: **−5.5% ROIC, capital-hungry** ✗
- Cash flow: **negative FCF, SBC-heavy** ✗
- Valuation & margin of safety: **$80.25 vs. conservative DCF $3.44–$8.38** ✗
- Inversion: **three compounding ways to lose** ✗

The operator deserves respect; the _price_ does not. This is a wonderful story at a wonderful price — the exact combination that produces mediocre outcomes for investors. I would not buy it here, and I would not be ashamed to miss the upside if the story works, because being right on this business at this price would require being lucky, and Munger's rule is to never compete in luck.

**Rating: D (Avoid).** Watch list only — a price meaningfully below the base-case intrinsic value (i.e., well under ~$20 on my conservative numbers, or a demonstrably lower risk profile via successful Neutron flight + clean Iridium integration) would begin to change the math. Today, there is no margin of safety.

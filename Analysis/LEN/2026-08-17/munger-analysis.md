# Charlie Munger Lens — Lennar Corporation (NYSE: LEN)

**Reference price $86.54 (2026-08-17 close). Market cap $20.85B. 240.90M shares.**[7]

## Bottom Line

**Rating: C — WATCH/HOLD at $86.54.** Lennar is the rare combination I like: a simple business I can explain to a 12-year-old (buy land, build a house, sell a mortgage with it), honest owner-minded management whose compensation is the best-structured I have seen in homebuilding, and the leanest balance sheet in the industry (15.8% homebuilding debt-to-capital, 0.1 years of owned land supply).[3][4][1] The trouble is entirely on the price side. TTM ROIC of 6.2% is a third of its FY22 peak and far below any sane cost of capital; at 13.45x *trough* EPS the market is paying the top of LEN's own five-year P/E band on the bottom of its earnings cycle, and at 0.96x book it is paying for a 15% ROE that has not existed for two years.[8][9] My conservative math — WACC +3% discount, terminal growth ≤2% — puts intrinsic value at **bear $47 / base $63 / bull $81, weighted ~$61**, roughly 30% *below* today's price. I would not buy at $86.54. I would be very interested below $63, greedy below $55, and all-in below $50 if the balance sheet stays as it is. "A great business at a fair price is superior to a fair business at a great price" — but this is a *good* cyclical business at a price that already assumes the recovery arrives. Watching, not buying.

## 1. Circle of Competence: Inside

The business is unchanged in essence since 1954: a family needs a house; there is a structural shortage of housing; local scale, purchasing power, and balance-sheet discipline decide who profits.[1] Lennar is the largest U.S. homebuilder by volume — 82,583 deliveries and $34.2B revenue in FY25 — and it attaches a mortgage/title machine that captures 83% of its buyers' financing.[3][2] The one real complexity — the options-heavy land-bank structure (Millrose spin-off Feb 2025, 98% of 495,466 homesites controlled via options, $7.06B of deposits) — is explained with unusual candor in the company's own June 2026 investor deck and on the Q2 call.[3][13] I understand the business, I understand the cycle, and I understand where the hidden costs sit. **Rating: Inside.** What I do *not* know is the direction of 30-year mortgage rates over the next two years — and neither does anyone else, which is why the price discipline below matters.

## 2. Mental Models Applied

**1. Regression to the mean (mathematics) — the bull and bear simultaneously.** Incentive spend is 12.9% of revenue against management's stated 4–6% normal; it has declined three quarters running (14.5% → 14.1% → 12.9%), and "incentives are declining, margins are starting to improve."[1][13] Every point of incentives handed back is roughly $300M of pre-tax profit — more than a year of buybacks. But regression cuts the other way on the *quality* axis: ROE has regressed from 22.8% (FY21) to 7.4% (TTM), and P/B of 0.96 only makes sense if ROE recovers above the ~10.5% cost of equity. The market's multiple assumes the good mean-reversion; my price waits for evidence.

**2. Incentives (psychology/economics) — the best part of this company.** Stuart Miller's salary of $1M has been frozen since 2003; his bonus is a 0.20% profit share on pretax income *after a 7.3% capital charge*, capped at $7M (he received $2.77M for FY25); 70% of his equity is performance-based; company-wide SBC is ~$139M TTM, ~0.4% of revenue.[4][2] This is a management that is paid to earn returns on capital, not to grow the balance sheet — exactly the incentive structure that produces the land-light model. The counter-incentive: a dual-class structure giving Miller ~42% of votes on ~8% of economics is a permanent license to make mistakes without consequence.[4]

**3. Opportunity cost (economics) — the buyback arithmetic.** Repurchases of $2.26B (FY24), $1.81B (FY25), and $737M (H1 FY26) at average prices around $89–123 retired shares at roughly 1.0x book while the business was earning ~7–8% on equity — a ~7–8% nominal return on the shareholders' money at a time when a riskless alternative paid ~4.7% and the company's own cost of equity is ~10.5%.[9][3] And in February 2026 Lennar *issued* $700M of 5.20% notes due 2030 while carrying $1.8B of homebuilding cash.[3] The direction is right — share count is down ~7.5% YoY — but the timing says the machine is on autopilot. At my buy zone ($55–63, ~0.65x book with normalization ahead), the same buyback dollar would earn 15%+ on normalized ROE.

**4. Ecological niche and symbiosis (biology).** Lennar's niche is the "manufacturing" of entry-level housing: record 121-day cycle time, construction cost of $81/sq ft (down 13% over two years), inventory turns of 2.5x vs 1.8x a year ago.[13][1] Its symbiosis with the land banks (including spun-off Millrose, ~$18.5B deployed on Lennar's behalf) is mutually beneficial only while volumes flow at an 86% delivery rate — and the symbiosis has a cost: roughly ~$270M/quarter of implied option-fee maintenance, mostly capitalized into ACOR rather than expensed.[13] A symbiosis with an 11% partner is cheaper than owning land through a downturn, but it is not free.

**5. Inversion on the P/B (a Munger special).** Justified P/B = ROE ÷ cost of equity. At TTM ROE 7.4% and a ~10.5% cost of equity, book is fairly valued at ~0.70x. Paying 0.96x book is not "cheap" on current economics; it is a bet that ROE re-rates to ~15%+ — a bet I take only with a discount for being wrong.

## 3. Inversion: How This Investment Fails

**Failure mode 1 — normalization is a mirage.** If "volume over margin" is a permanent strategy rather than a cycle (entry-level affordability as the new steady state), then LEN deserves its 4.9% net margin, TTM earnings are normal, and the fair price is 8–10x $6.43 = **$51–64**. The stock is 35–70% too high in that world.[8][7]

**Failure mode 2 — the land bank's hidden rent never recedes.** ~$18.5B of land-bank capital at ~11% cost of capital is a multi-billion-dollar annual economic rent embedded in future cost of sales. If that structure's cost does not decline with rates, normalized gross margins land a few points *below* the old on-balance-sheet era, and my base-case $63 is too generous.[13][3]

**Failure mode 3 — a recession meets a 14% cancellation rate.** Q2 cancellations were 14.0%; backlog value $6.6B at ASP $393k is only ~9 months of revenue at the FY26 pace. A 2008-style demand shock would convert the $7.06B of option deposits into forfeiture losses — the land-light model's version of land write-downs.[1][3]

**Failure mode 4 — the capital-allocation autopilot.** Buy high ($89–123), borrow at 5.20% to stay liquid, and hold $1.8B cash — this is not disaster, but a company buying its own stock at 1.0x book with 7% ROE while issuing debt is destroying a little value every quarter and calling it returning capital.[3][4]

**Failure mode 5 — governance/key-person.** A 68-year-old architect-CEO with 42% of the votes, 8% of the economics, and no named successor (co-CEO Jon Jaffe retired 12/31/25 with no replacement planned). The company's whole strategy is Stuart Miller's strategy. [4][3]

**Bias self-check (from the 25):** *Anchoring* — at $86.54 vs a 52-week high of $144.24, "down 40%" feels cheap; I have explicitly refused that anchor and valued from earnings. *Confirmation bias* — I want to like this business (simple, honest, buybacks); the Berkshire-buys-homebuilders headlines flatter that view; Berkshire's purchase is not a reason and I will not use it as one. *Loss aversion* — the reflex is to buy the dip; the discipline is to wait for $63. *Social proof* — 8 of 18 analysts say Hold, average target $86.23 ≈ price; consensus is not a margin of safety.[7]

**Avoid-stupidity checklist:** Don't chase at high valuations — TTM P/E 13.45 is at the *top* of LEN's five-year band (5.5–15.6x) on trough earnings ✓ flagged. Don't buy companies whose moat has not shown in returns ✓ flagged (4.9% net margin). Don't invest in complex models ✓ the land banks are complex *but disclosed*. Don't buy mediocre-at-cheap ✓ not mediocre, but cyclical. Don't invest outside competence ✓ inside. **The only stupidity left on the table is paying $86.54 for it.**

## 5. Earnings-Quality Tests

- **OCF vs NI: FAIL (cyclically).** TTM OCF $883M vs NI $1,599M = **0.55x** against the >1.2x standard — H1 FY26 consumed $718M building spring inventory (seasonal), and even in the good FY24 it was 0.57x. Homebuilders' earnings ride inventory accounting; the option model keeps most of that inventory off-balance-sheet, so the *quality* question is about the off-book part.[2][8]
- **The real accounting issue: capitalized land-bank rent.** ~$18.5B of land-bank capital at ~11% cost; analysts on the Q2 call flagged ~$270M/quarter of implied option-fee maintenance flowing into "deposits and pre-acquisition costs" ($7.06B) rather than the income statement. Miller's answer — "a natural ebb and flow of capital… it will ultimately equalize" — is honest about mechanics but means *current* margins overstate the true economic run-rate.[13][3]
- **SBC: excellent.** $139M TTM (~0.4% of revenue) is immaterial; options grants are essentially absent; equity is performance-based.[2][4]
- **CapEx: negligible.** ~$166M TTM against ~$140M D&A — the plant is the trades; no maintenance-capex games.[8]
- **Goodwill: watchful.** $3,442M = 16% of equity (below the 30% alarm), legacy acquisitions; no current impairment trigger, but it exists because past deals were done at cycle highs.[3]
- **Leverage & coverage: strong.** HB debt/capital 15.8%, net HB debt 9.4%; estimated interest ~$200M vs OI $2,148M → coverage >10x (est.); no revolver borrowings; $4.9B liquidity.[1][3]
- **Owner earnings (TTM):** NI $1,599M + D&A ~$140M − capex $166M − WC build ≈ **$1.55B ≈ $6.4/share** — essentially EPS, which is the honest starting point for the DCF below. Normalized (incentives back to ~6%): **~$10–10.5/share** (est.).[2][3]

## 6. Valuation: Conservative DCF

Per Munger doctrine: discount = WACC + 3% margin, terminal growth ≤ 2%. WACC est. ~10.5% (rf ~4.7% + ERP ~4.3% × beta 1.40 ≈ 10.7%, modest debt benefit) → **central discount 13.5%**. Ten-year owner-earnings model on $6.4/share TTM.

| Scenario | Path (owner earnings, 10 yrs) | r | tg | **IV/share** |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Conservative | $6.4 flat forever (incentives stay ≥10%, rates >6.5%) | 14.0% | 1.5% | **~$47** |
| Neutral | $6.4 → $9.5 (+4%/yr; incentives normalize to ~6% by 2029) | 13.5% | 2.0% | **~$63** |
| Optimistic | $6.4 → $10.5 by yr 5, then +3%/yr (rates <5.5% by 2028) | 13.0% | 2.0% | **~$81** |

**Cross-checks.** (a) Multiple of normalized owner earnings: base $63 ≈ **6x** normalized $10.5 — the multiple a 13.5% hurdle supports. (b) P/B logic: bull $81 ≈ 0.9x book *after* ROE recovers to ~15%; bear $47 ≈ 0.65x book × trough $6.4. (c) Relative: LEN 13.45x TTM is the top of its own 5.5–15.6x five-year band and the stock carries the *lowest* TTM net margin of the big four (4.9% vs DHI 9.1%, PHM 11.6%, NVR 11.8%) — the market is paying a normalized multiple for pre-normalization earnings.[9][10][11][12]

**Margin of safety: FAILS.** Price $86.54 vs base $63 = +37% (the market cap exceeds my base IV by ~$5.7B). Against weighted $61, the expected value is **−29%**. Munger's rule — intrinsic value above price by 30–40% — is inverted here: the price is ~40% above value. Dividend (2.31%) and buybacks soften but do not reverse that.

## 8. Risks

1. **Rate persistence / no margin normalization.** 30-yr fixed ~6.4–6.5%, Fed on hold at 3.50–3.75%; FY26 deliveries cut to 82–83k; EPS −36% YoY in Q2. If incentives stay double-digits, my $47 bear is the *base*.[1][13]
2. **Land-bank rent compression.** The ~11% cost on ~$18.5B of partner capital is the hidden tax on the land-light model; if it doesn't fall, normalized margins are structurally lower than the pre-2022 era.[13]
3. **Cycle + cancellation risk.** 14% cancellations; a demand shock turns $7.06B of option deposits into write-downs.[1][3]
4. **Capital-allocation autopilot.** Buybacks at $89–123 into falling margins; $700M of 5.20% notes issued with $1.8B cash on hand; small, steady value leakage.[3][4]
5. **Governance/key-person.** Dual-class control (42% votes / 8% economics); 68-year-old CEO with no successor named; the strategy is one man's thesis.[4]
6. **Multiple-compression to mean-ROE book.** If ROE stays 7–8%, justified P/B is ~0.70x → **$60–65** on no news at all — which is exactly my weighted IV.[9]

## 9. Final Rating: C — WATCH/HOLD

The company passes nearly every quality test I care about — except the one that compounds: demonstrated return on capital. ROIC of 6.2% is not a moat; it is a moat in waiting. The management is aligned, candid, and allocating in the right direction; the balance sheet would survive anything short of a 2008. But the price pays today for a normalization that is promised, not delivered, and my conservative arithmetic puts fair value ~30% below the quote. I wait. **Buy zone $55–63 (starter at $63, accumulate at $55, conviction below $50); hold $63–90; reduce ≥$90.** If Q3 (Sept 2026) prints gross margin above ~17% with incentives below 12%, my base rises and the first tranche begins; if rates fall toward 5.5%, my bull case becomes the base. Patience is the edge.

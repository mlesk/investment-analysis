# Warren Buffett Lens — Lennar Corporation (NYSE: LEN)

**Reference price $86.54 (2026-08-17 close). Market cap $20.85B. 240.90M shares.**[7]

## Bottom Line

**Rating: B — Buy, but price-contingent.** Lennar is a simple business I understand — it buys dirt, nails sticks to it, and sells a mortgage along with the house — run by an owner-minded, candid management that has spent three hard years rebuilding the company into the leanest balance sheet in homebuilding (2% of land owned, 15.8% homebuilding debt-to-total-capital, $4.9B liquidity).[3][1] The moat is narrow, not wide: no pricing power, and TTM net margin of 4.9% is the lowest of the big four builders (DHI 9.1%, PHM 11.6%, NVR 11.8%).[8][10][11][12] But earnings are cyclically depressed — incentives of 12.9% vs. management's stated normalized 4–6% sit entirely inside the income statement — and at $86.54 the market is paying ~6.2x what I estimate to be mid-cycle earnings power (~$11.5/share), 0.96x book value, with a 2.31% dividend and a 6.3% buyback yield underneath.[1][8][9] I estimate intrinsic value at roughly **$78 / $115 / $148 (bear/base/bull), weighted ~$116**. That is a ~25% discount to base, not my customary 30–50% — so this is a "wonderful-ish business at a fair-ish price," worth buying with both hands below $80, worth owning with patience here, and worth selling above $120. At today's price I would hold, not chase.

## 1. Business & Circle of Competence

Lennar, founded 1954 and headquartered in Miami, is the largest U.S. homebuilder by volume: it acquires and develops residential land (now mostly *optioned*, not owned), builds single-family homes under the Lennar brand across East, Central, South Central, and West segments, and runs an attached mortgage/title business that captures 83% of Lennar homebuyers' mortgages.[3][2] FY2025: 82,583 deliveries, $34.2B revenue; Q2 FY2026 (ended 5/31/26): 20,519 deliveries at a $371k average price, 1,683 active communities.[4][1]

This is squarely inside my circle of competence. The business has not changed in essence since 1954: a family needs a house; a shortage of housing persists; local scale, buying power, and balance-sheet discipline decide who profits. "The fundamental shortage of housing in America has not been solved," as Stuart Miller puts it.[1] Will people need new homes in 10 years? The U.S. is structurally underbuilt; entry-level inventory is scarce; yes. Will technology disrupt the act of building? It improves costs and cycle times (Lennar's own "Lennar Machine" — record 121-day cycle time, construction cost of $81/sq ft, down 7% YoY and 13% over two years) but does not replace the local land-position-plus-construction business.[13][3] The only meaningful complexity — an options-heavy land-bank structure (Millrose, "land banks"), a mortgage warehouse segment, small multifamily/tech investments — is disclosed with unusual clarity in the new investor deck management published in June 2026.[1] This is a business I can value.

## 2. Economic Moat (Four Types)

**Intangible assets — weak-to-modest.** "Lennar" carries some consumer trust and its *Everything's Included®* value platform is a genuine differentiator among first-time buyers, but no buyer pays a premium because the home says Lennar; they pay a market-clearing price set by comparables. No patents, no meaningful regulatory barriers to homebuilding entry.[13][3]

**Switching costs — none.** A homebuyer's "switching cost" is a $500 earnest deposit.

**Network effects — none.**

**Cost advantages — real but narrow.** This is the entire moat, and it has three layers: (1) *Scale purchasing/construction economics* — the largest builder buys materials and sets trade terms at the best prices; construction costs down 13% over two years while volume held.[1][13] (2) *Capital-efficiency advantage* — the land-light model (98% of 495,466 homesites controlled via options; 2% owned; 0.1 years of owned supply) means Lennar carries almost no land risk and funds growth from operations; land banks (including spun-off Millrose) deliver finished lots just-in-time at an 86% delivery rate.[3][13] (3) *Manufacturing consistency* — even-flow production, 121-day cycle time, inventory turn of 2.5x vs. 1.8x a year ago.[1] Crucially, the moat's economic proof is *not* yet in margins: TTM net margin is 4.9% vs. DHI 9.1%, PHM 11.6%, NVR 11.8%, and Q2 gross margin of 15.6% compares to PHM's 25% and DHI's 20.7%.[8][10][11][12][13] Lennar has chosen to convert its cost advantage into *volume and affordability*, not margin. The strategy is coherent — cement scale now, harvest when incentives normalize — but until normalized margins (20%+) actually arrive, the moat earns a **Narrow** rating. A moat that hasn't yet shown up in returns is a hope, not a fact.

## 3. Management

**Integrity — high.** Stuart Miller (Executive Chairman, CEO and President; sole CEO since Jon Jaffe's retirement on 12/31/25, with no replacement planned) has run the company with his father's DNA since 1997. The letters and calls are unusually candid about a down cycle: "persistently elevated mortgage rates, constrained affordability, and cautious consumer sentiment"; "traffic is inconsistent; intent is high; urgency to close is measured and deliberate rather than confident and energized."[1][13][4] Governance is mostly strong — 8 of 9 directors independent, lead director, clawback policy, no hedging, no options grants, 88% say-on-pay.[4] Two real flags: (1) dual-class structure (Class B = 10 votes) gives Miller ~42% of voting power on ~8% of the economics — a permanent, unaccountable control premium, though Miller's 70.2% of Class B and long record blunt the worst-case reading; (2) key-person dependence on a 68-year-old founder-CEO.[4] On balance, owner-operators with skin in the game beat hired hands.

**Capital allocation — strong, one timing ding.** The record over the last 2.5 years: $2.26B buybacks in FY24, $1.81B in FY25 (plus 8.0M shares retired via the Millrose exchange offer), $737M in H1 FY26; dividend doubled from $1.00 (2021) to $2.00; $400M notes redeemed with cash in June 2026; Millrose land spun off to shareholders (Feb 2025); Quarterra multifamily majority sold to TPG (Dec 2025); Rausch Coleman acquired (~$254M net) into the 2025 trough to expand affordable-market share.[9][3][4] The ding: much of the buyback was executed at $89–123 (FY25–H1'26 averages), well above today's $86.54 — the company paid up while margins were still falling, and the Feb-2026 decision to issue $700M of 5.20% notes while carrying $1.8B of homebuilding cash suggests the "conservative on buybacks while ACOR imbalance persists" discipline Miller describes on the call.[3][13] Remaining authorization is $1.0B as of 5/31/26.[3]

**Operational — excellent.** Cost/sq ft down 13% in two years; record cycle time; inventory per community cut from 3 to 2.1 homes; incentive spend down three quarters running (14.5% → 14.1% → 12.9%) — "incentives are declining, margins are starting to improve."[1][13] Compensation is unusually shareholder-friendly in *structure*: Miller's $1M salary has been frozen since 2003, his bonus is a 0.20% profit-share on pretax income after a 7.3% capital charge (capped at $7M — he received $2.77M for FY25), and 70% of his equity is performance-based with target payouts at the 65th percentile.[4] Magnitude remains rich ($29.5M total FY25) but SBC company-wide is only ~$139M TTM, ~0.4% of revenue.[4][2] **Management score: 8/10.**

## 4. Financial Health & Earnings Quality

**Balance sheet — fortress-grade for a builder.** Homebuilding debt $4.05B = 15.8% of total capital; net HB debt $2.23B = 9.4%; no revolver borrowings on the $3.1B facility; $1.7B term loan due May 2028; next bond maturity June 2027; total liquidity $4.9B.[3][1] The land-light model means the classic builder killer — a land-heavy balance sheet in a downturn — is largely gone: owned land $10.9B (down from $11.4B YoY) with owned homesites representing just 0.1 years of supply, while 484k lots sit off-balance-sheet in options.[3][13] Covenant headroom is enormous (achieved leverage 14.7% vs. 60% max; liquidity test 30.0x vs. 1.00 minimum).[3]

**Profitability trend** (ROE / ROIC, per stockanalysis): FY21 22.8%/17.3% → FY22 20.6%/20.8% → FY23 15.6%/16.3% → FY24 14.5%/14.8% → FY25 8.4%/8.2% → TTM 7.4%/6.2%.[9] The 15%-ROE standard has been violated for two years — but this is incentive math, not franchise decay: roughly 7–9 points of gross margin are currently given away in incentives versus the stated 4–6% normal.[1] On the balance sheet actually deployed, returns are better than the P&L suggests (19.7% return on inventory in FY25, 15.3% in Q2).[4][13]

**Cash flow — cyclical but real.** OCF TTM $883M, FCF TTM $717M; H1 FY26 consumed $718M building spring inventory (seasonally normal); FY24 FCF was $2.23B when margins were healthy.[2][8] CapEx is trivial (~$166M TTM, ~0.5% of revenue) — the "plant" is the trades.[8] Dividends ($247M H1) and buybacks are funded without debt.

**Earnings quality — one honest flag.** SBC is immaterial; goodwill ($3.44B, 16% of equity) stems from old acquisitions and carries modest impairment risk but no current trigger.[3] The flag is the *capitalization of land-bank option maintenance fees* into "deposits and pre-acquisition costs" ($7.06B). The land banks have deployed ~$18.5B on Lennar's behalf; at their ~11% cost of capital, that is a multi-billion-dollar annual economic rent that is mostly capitalized rather than expensed as it accrues (analysts on the Q2 call flagged ~$270M/quarter of implied maintenance vs. expense). Miller's answer is honest about the mechanics — "a natural ebb and flow of capital… it will ultimately equalize" — but a Buffett lens must price the model at the *economic* cost: normalized margins after option fees will be a few points below the pre-2022 on-balance-sheet era.[13] I have set mid-cycle gross margin at ~20.5% (vs. 22.5% in FY24, 23.9% in FY23) to reflect this.

## 5. Growth Prospects

Industry growth is structural but slow: housing is short by millions of units, demographics keep forming households, and federal attention on affordability is "genuinely unprecedented."[13] Company growth is deliberate: unit volume held ~82–83k in a hostile rate environment (30-yr fixed at 6.4–6.5%, Fed on hold at 3.50–3.75%, May headline CPI 4.2% on energy) — and management's entire strategy is to be the low-cost producer positioned to harvest share and margin when rates normalize.[13][1] Community count +4.1% to 1,683; backlog +8.2% to 16,818 homes.[1] The near-term growth curve points down (FY26 deliveries cut to 82–83k; Q3 EPS guide $1.20–1.40 vs. $1.24 last year), but the earnings-recovery curve — incentives 12.9% → 4–6% — is worth far more than any volume growth.[1][3] This is a "same-store margin recovery + slow share gains" story, not a compounder.

## 6. Owner Earnings & DCF

**Owner earnings (TTM):** NI $1,599M + D&A ~$140M − capex $166M − working-capital build ≈ **$1.55B ≈ $6.4/share** — roughly equal to EPS, because homebuilders' working capital is inventory that the option model now keeps off-book.[2][3] Normalized owner-earnings power at ~20.5% gross margin: ~$2.6B ≈ **$10.5/share** (my estimate).

**Ten-year owner-earnings DCF** (discount 10%, terminal growth 2.5%; values per share):

| Scenario | Path | Terminal value | **IV/share** |
|---|---|---|---|
| Conservative | $6.4 flat through the decade (incentives stay 10%+, land-bank rent persists, rates stay >6%) | 1.025 × $6.4 / 0.075 | **~$78** |
| Neutral | $6.4 growing 5%/yr as incentives normalize toward 6% and costs keep falling | 1.025 × $10.6 / 0.075 | **~$105** |

Cross-check with mid-cycle earnings multiples: bear 9x $8.0 = $72; base 10x $11.5 = $115; bull 10.5x $14.5 = $152. **Final: bear $78 / base $115 / bull $148.**

Lennar is the cheapest of the big four on book (0.96x, with BVPS ~$90), carries the highest combined cash-return yield (dividend 2.31% + buyback yield 6.34%), and its historical P/E ran 5.5–15.6x over the last five years — today's 13.45x *trough* EPS is the market paying the top of LEN's historical band on the bottom of its earnings cycle, a classic cyclical-value setup.[9] EV/EBITDA 11.0x TTM vs. peers' mid-single digits in good years.[9] On *normalized* EPS the stock is at ~7.5x — cheap, but not absurdly so, because the normal level itself is debated. ## 8. Margin of Safety Verdict

At $86.54 vs. base $115, the discount is ~25% — short of my 30–50% hurdle. Against the bear case ($78), today's price offers *negative* protection; against the bull it is trivial. The honest Buffett arithmetic: this is a fair-to-slightly-cheap price for a quality business whose earnings are still falling (Q3 guide $1.20–1.40 EPS; H2 FY26 will print ~$2.5 of annualized EPS, putting the stock at ~30x run-rate on trough economics).[13] I do not require a purchase at exactly the bottom — the moat, management, and balance sheet justify holding a starter position into weakness. **Buy below $80 (30%+ discount to base); conviction below $70; reduce above $120; avoid above $140.** It is also worth noting the company's most famous investor-adjacent signal: Berkshire Hathaway has been *buying homebuilders* through 2026 (its Q2-26 13F showed increased homebuilder stakes, per market reports), which does not make LEN a buy — but confirms the sector's economics currently satisfy a famously demanding buyer.[7][10]

## 9. Risks

1. **Rates stay high, earnings keep falling.** 30-yr fixed 6.4–6.5%, Fed on hold, energy-driven CPI re-acceleration — the Q2/Q3 prints show EPS down ~30% YoY and guidance cut again. A recession would pressure volume and 14% cancellation rates.[13][1]
2. **The land-light model's hidden rent.** ~$18.5B of land-bank capital at ~11% cost, mostly capitalized into ACOR, is a real drag on future margins; if the land banks' cost of capital does not come down, normalized margins land lower than my base ~20.5%.[13][3]
3. **$7.06B of option deposits at risk.** Forfeitable deposits are the new "land write-downs" — manageable, but a sharp downturn still converts them to losses.[3]
4. **Governance/key-person.** Dual-class control (Miller ~42% of votes on ~8% of economics); no obvious successor to a 68-year-old architect-CEO.[4]
5. **Normalization may be a mirage.** If the affordability-driven "volume over margin" strategy is permanent rather than cyclical, LEN deserves its 4.9% net margin and today's price is fully valued.[1][8]
6. **Sector multiple risk.** At 0.96x book with trough ROE, further multiple compression toward historical deep-trough levels (0.7–0.85x book) implies $65–75 even with no earnings change.[9][7]

## 10. Final Rating: **B — Buy (price-contingent)**

A simple, permanent business; a narrow but real cost-and-capital moat; owner-minded, candid, allocator-skilled management; a fortress balance sheet; and a price that embeds the bear case — but with only ~25% margin of safety to my base case and earnings still falling. Buy on weakness below $80, hold patiently at $86.54, sell into strength above $120. This is a stock to buy in the fear the market has been selling for two years — and to buy *bigger* when it gets more afraid.
